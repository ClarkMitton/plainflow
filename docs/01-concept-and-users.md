# Concept and users

## The idea in one paragraph

Most people who want to use a new tool with personal data have no idea what they are supposed to do about it, and the people who do know (DPOs) are too busy to sit with each of them. The canvas lets a governance lead sit next to someone, build what they want to do as a picture in a few minutes, and have the picture tell both of them what it needs. The paperwork is derived from the picture, not written afterwards. Flipping a lever shows the cost of each choice, so the safer version of an idea becomes a visible option rather than a lecture.

## Three users

| User | What they want | What they leave with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The person with the idea (teacher, manager, small business owner) | Can I do this? What do I need? How long? | A plain verdict: go, go once these three things are done, or not like this but here is the nearest version that works. Plus the one change that makes it easier, and a one-page action plan. |
| The governance lead sitting beside them | Understand the flow fast, show the cost of each choice, hand something on | The flow saved as JSON, the pack generated, nothing to retype |
| The DPO or sign-off person | A complete, consistent description they can trust without redoing it | Screening answers with reasons, each company's Article 28 position with evidence, and a clear list of what the canvas could not answer |

The tool fails the first user if they leave with a list of documents and no answer.

## The jobs, in order of how often they happen

1. **Check something we already use.** Most real sessions. Someone has been using a tool for a year and has just realised they should have asked. No launch date, retrospective.
2. **Screen a new idea** in under fifteen minutes with the person present.
3. **Compare two ways of doing it** (pin A, try B) so the safer version is a choice.
4. **Reuse what's known.** A company card filled once serves every flow that touches that company.
5. **Explain an existing tool** to someone who inherited it: what goes where, who holds it.
6. **Generate the paperwork** from the canvas.
7. **Re-check when something changes.** A lever flips, only the affected rows reopen.
8. **Keep a register.** Every saved flow is an entry in the record of processing.

## Two modes, one engine

- **Flow first.** Build the picture; the tier is derived; the requirements follow.
- **Tier first.** Pick the tier at the top; the palette greys out what that tier can't do (Tier 2 can't connect to a non-sanctioned company, Tier 3 can't leave the boundary). Design inside the fence.

Same rules table. Tier is an input or an output. A switch in the top bar flips it.

## The three questions every flow has to answer

The DPIA conversation always mixes these up. The canvas separates them into node families:

1. **Who is doing what with the data.** Controller, processor, joint controller, sub-processor. The ICO test is who decides purpose and means. An organisation isn't inherently one or the other; the role is a property of the flow, not the company. A processor that steps outside instructions becomes a controller for that step.
2. **What the data is and about whom.** The tier (0 to 3), plus the one split that matters most: is it about how a named person is doing.
3. **What the processing does to people.** The nine screening criteria: scoring, automated decisions, monitoring, special category, large scale, matching, vulnerable subjects, innovative tech, blocking a right or service.

The documents are just the output of those three layers.

## What it is not

- Not legal advice. It screens and drafts; it never declares a flow lawful.
- Not a full DPIA tool. Steps 3, 5 and 6 of the ICO process (consultation, risk scoring, measures) stay human. The canvas pre-fills the rest.
- Not a general whiteboard. Five node types, a few levers each, and nothing you can't derive something from.
